C H A P T E R   1
Why AI Governance Is Now a Board Issue
The decision this chapter supports. Whether to treat AI governance as a compliance line item delegated downward, or
as a standing item of board-level risk oversight with dedicated ownership and budget. The evidence assembled here
supports the second answer.
In this chapter. The enforcement record that moved AI from a technology question to a governance question; the two-sided
regulatory reality (new AI-specific law layered on old law that already applies); the asymmetry between the cost of proactive
governance and the cost of the documented failures; and the affirmative business case that has emerged alongside the
defensive one.
1.1 The enforcement record is no longer hypothetical
For most of the last decade, AI risk was discussed in the conditional tense: what could go wrong, what a regulator might one
day do. That era is over. The record that now exists is a record of completed enforcement actions, court rulings, settlements,
and resignations, and it spans every category of consequence a board is charged with overseeing — financial, legal,
operational, and reputational.
Consider what the documented record already contains. In the United States, the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission settled its first AI-related hiring discrimination case in 2023: iTutorGroup agreed to pay $365,000 after the
agency alleged that its application software automatically rejected female applicants aged 55 and older and male applicants
aged 60 and older. The dollar figure was modest; the precedent was not. It established that an algorithm’s screening
decision is the employer’s screening decision, and that intent is not required for liability. The point was reinforced when a
federal court in California allowed Mobley v. Workday to proceed — a discrimination action against a software vendor whose
AI screening tools were alleged to disadvantage applicants on the basis of age, race, and disability. The court’s willingness
to treat an AI vendor as potentially liable in the hiring chain redrew the risk map for every company that buys, rather than
builds, its AI.
Financial regulators have moved as well. In October 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau ordered Apple and
Goldman Sachs to pay a combined sum exceeding $89 million in penalties and consumer redress over failures in the
Apple Card program, including dispute-handling breakdowns rooted in the automated systems that processed customer
interactions. The same program had earlier been investigated by the New York State Department of Financial Services after
public allegations of gender bias in credit limits; the regulator found no unlawful discrimination under fair lending law, but
its report faulted the program’s transparency and customer service — a reminder that an algorithm can survive a bias
inquiry and still generate an enforcement file. Meanwhile the Securities and Exchange Commission opened a new front in
2024 with its first “AI washing” actions, charging two investment advisers — Delphia and Global Predictions — with
making false and misleading statements about their use of AI, and collecting $400,000 in combined civil penalties. The
message to public-company boards was unambiguous: statements about AI capability are now securities statements.
In Europe, the enforcement machinery predates the AI Act, because the GDPR already reaches most of what AI systems do
with personal data. The Irish Data Protection Commission’s €1.2 billion fine against Meta in May 2023 — the largest GDPR
fine on record, concerning unlawful transfers of European user data to the United States — demonstrated the scale the
regime can reach. AI-specific applications followed: the Italian data protection authority, the Garante, temporarily banned
ChatGPT in 2023 pending privacy remediations and in December 2024 fined OpenAI €15 million over the legal basis and
transparency of its data processing. Biometric AI has proven especially costly: Clearview AI, which scraped billions of facial
images to build a recognition database, has accumulated multi-million-euro fines across European authorities and agreed to
a settlement valued at roughly $50 million in United States litigation under Illinois’s Biometric Information Privacy Act.

Korea’s enforcement history is shorter but pointed, and it arrived early. In April 2021, the Personal Information Protection
Commission fined Scatter Lab, developer of the conversational chatbot “Lee Luda” (Iruda), 103.3 million won for
building the service on private KakaoTalk messages collected through its earlier apps for a different purpose — the
country’s first sanction against an AI service for personal-data misuse — and its corrective orders reached the unlawfully
processed data itself, not merely the balance sheet. The case fixed the PIPC’s posture years before Korea’s AI Basic Act
existed: training data is regulated data, remedies can reach the model, and a consumer backlash can end a product faster
than any regulator. The service was withdrawn within weeks of launch.
The record extends beyond fines to the failures that end careers and governments. The Dutch childcare benefits scandal
— in which an algorithmic risk-scoring system used by the tax administration wrongly accused thousands of families,
disproportionately those with dual nationality, of fraud — contributed to the resignation of the entire Dutch cabinet in
January 2021 and remains the canonical demonstration that algorithmic harm scales administratively: one flawed model,
applied uniformly, manufactures injustice at a volume no human process could match. In the United States, the documented
wrongful arrests of Robert Williams and Porcha Woodruff in Detroit, both driven by erroneous facial recognition matches,
put faces on the same lesson.

The new layer is nonetheless consequential, and its calendar has just been redrawn. The EU AI Act — Regulation (EU)
2024/1689, the world’s first comprehensive horizontal AI statute — entered into force in August 2024 with staggered
application: prohibitions on certain practices from February 2025, obligations for general-purpose AI model providers from
August 2025, and the demanding high-risk regime originally scheduled for August 2026. In July 2026, the Digital Omnibus
on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) deferred the high-risk obligations — to December 2, 2027 for standalone Annex III
systems and to August 2, 2028 for AI embedded in products already covered by EU product-safety law — while leaving the
prohibitions, the general-purpose AI obligations, and the Article 50 transparency and AI-content-labeling duties on their
original dates. Chapter 4 treats the details. What matters at board level is the shape of the situation: the deferral bought
implementation time, not exemption, and the penalty ceiling that awaits — up to €35 million or 7 percent of global
annual turnover for prohibited practices — sits above even the GDPR’s 4 percent maximum.

In the United States, no federal AI act exists, and none is imminent. What exists instead is enforcement by interpretation —
the FTC, CFPB, EEOC, SEC, and state attorneys general applying their existing statutes to AI conduct — plus an
accelerating layer of state legislation, from Colorado’s first-in-the-nation comprehensive AI act to New York City’s bias-audit
mandate for automated hiring tools. Chapters 6 and 7 map that terrain. The governance implication is the same on both
continents: an organization cannot wait for regulatory certainty, because the operative rules already apply, and the new
ones arrive on dates that are known.
Korea completed the trio in January 2026. The AI Basic Act (Act No. 20676) and its Enforcement Decree took effect on
January 22, 2026, making Korea the second jurisdiction after the EU with a comprehensive horizontal AI statute — one
that applies extraterritorially to foreign operators whose AI products or services affect the Korean market. The Act imposes
advance-notice and labeling duties on generative AI, lifecycle safety and reliability obligations on “high-impact” AI in
domains such as employment, healthcare, finance, and public services, and additional safety duties on “high-performance”
AI above a compute threshold of 1026 FLOPs. Administrative fines are modest — capped at 30 million won — and the
Ministry of Science and ICT is operating a grace period of at least one year, deferring fines except in cases of serious social
harm. But the structural signal outweighs the penalty arithmetic: for any company serving Korean users, AI governance is
now a market-entry condition, larger foreign operators must designate a domestic representative, and Korea’s privacy
regulator has already shown that its remedies can reach the model itself. Chapter 8 treats the regime in full.

Named research adds a quantified dimension on the security-adjacent portion of the risk. IBM’s Cost of a Data Breach
Report 2025 found that breaches involving ungoverned, unsanctioned “shadow AI” carried materially higher costs than the
global average, and that a substantial share of organizations suffering AI-related security incidents lacked basic AI access
controls and governance policies — a direct, measured link between the absence of governance and the size of the loss.
Stanford University’s AI Index has documented a sustained year-over-year rise in publicly reported AI incidents alongside
a sharp acceleration in legislative activity, which is to say: the hazard rate and the enforcement capacity are rising together.
On the cost-of-governance side, the required investment is real but bounded and largely front-loaded: an inventory and
classification exercise, a governance function with named ownership, assessment and testing processes proportionate to
risk tiers, and an evidence layer. Part III of this book prices and sequences that build over twelve months. The asymmetry is
structural: governance cost is planned, incremental, and capitalizable into every subsequent AI deployment, while failure
cost is unplanned, concentrated, and compounding. One is a budget line; the other is a contingency you do not control.
1.4 The affirmative case
The defensive case would suffice, but it understates the position, because governance has become a commercial capability.
Three mechanisms drive this. First, procurement: enterprise buyers, prompted by their own regulatory exposure, now
interrogate their vendors’ AI governance as a condition of contract, and a documented, audit-ready program shortens sales
cycles that an undocumented one stalls. Second, deployment velocity: organizations with a functioning classification and
gating process approve low-risk AI uses quickly and confidently, while organizations without one either freeze all adoption
out of generalized fear or approve everything out of ignorance — both of which are competitive failures. Third, capital
markets and M&A: AI governance has entered diligence checklists, and the ability to produce a system inventory, risk
classifications, and assessment records on request is now a component of enterprise value, much as security certifications
did a decade ago.
The chapter’s conclusion is the book’s premise: AI governance belongs on the board agenda not because regulation
demands ritual attention, but because the organization’s exposure and its opportunity now run through the same set of
systems, and both are governable. The next chapter supplies the framework for governing them.
The documented record through 2026 includes a €1.2 billion data protection fine, an $89 million consumer
finance order, multi-million-dollar biometric settlements, the first AI-discrimination settlements and vendor-
liability rulings in US employment law, securities penalties for overstated AI claims, and a governmental
collapse triggered by an algorithm. Every one of these was preventable at the governance layer.
C O S T O F I N A C T I O N
Treating AI governance as an annual policy-approval exercise. The documented failures in this chapter were
not caused by absent policies; they were caused by absent inventories, absent owners, absent testing, and
absent evidence. A policy without those four is a press release.
