7.1 Colorado: the case study of 2026
Colorado’s SB 24-205, signed May 17, 2024, was the first comprehensive state AI act and the closest American cousin of the
EU model: a duty of reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination, risk management programs, impact assessments,
and attorney-general reporting for high-risk systems in consequential decisions. It never took effect. Its start date was
pushed from February 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026; in April 2026 xAI sued on constitutional grounds and the Department of
Justice intervened in support; on April 27, 2026 a federal magistrate judge stayed enforcement; and on May 14, 2026,
Governor Polis signed SB 26-189, repealing and replacing the original act with a substantially narrower framework —
advance notice, post-decision disclosures, and consumer rights around automated decision-making technology (ADMT)
used in consequential decisions, enforced by the attorney general — effective January 1, 2027, with the original act’s duty
of care, risk-management mandates, and impact-assessment obligations eliminated.

The most ambitious AI statute in the United States was repealed seven weeks before its effective date.
Figure 7.1. The Colorado arc, 2024–2027. The capabilities the original act demanded —
inventory, disclosure, bias testing — survived into its replacement and into every other
regime; only the statutory superstructure changed.
Three lessons transfer beyond Colorado. First, the EU-style comprehensive model is, for now, in retreat at the US state level
— the surviving statutes cluster around transparency, disclosure, and specific prohibited uses rather than full management-
system mandates. Second, federal pressure is a real variable: the December 2025 executive order and DOJ’s intervention
shaped the outcome. Third — and most important for planning — the organizations that had built inventories, classifications,

and disclosure capability lost nothing when the statute changed, because those capabilities are what the replacement law
requires and what every other regime requires too. The companies that lost effort were those that had built to one statute’s
paperwork instead of to the underlying capability.

7.3 Planning against volatility
The temptation this landscape invites is paralysis — why build against statutes that may be repealed, stayed, or preempted?
The answer is that the volatility is concentrated in the statutory superstructure, not in the underlying demands. Across
every version of every state law in the table, the same four capabilities recur: knowing which systems make or shape
consequential decisions; telling affected people that automation is involved; being able to explain and review an adverse
outcome; and testing for discriminatory effect. Those are, respectively, Know, Prove, Control, and Control again — and they
are simultaneously what the EU requires, what Korea requires, and what federal agencies already enforce under old law.
Build the capability, and statutory churn becomes a mapping exercise; build to a single statute’s forms, and every
amendment is a fire drill.
