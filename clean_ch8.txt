Korea: The AI Basic Act and PIPA
The decision this chapter supports. Whether and how to treat Korea as a first-order governance jurisdiction — which, for
any organization with Korean users, revenue, or operations, it now is: the world’s second comprehensive horizontal AI
regime is in force there today.
In this chapter. A structured entry on the AI Basic Act; the three regulated categories — generative, high-impact, and
high-performance AI; the grace period and what it does not suspend; the domestic-representative mechanism; and the
privacy layer under PIPA and the PIPC.
8.1 The regime in one view
Instrument
Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Establishment of a Foundation for
Trust (AI Basic Act), Act No. 20676, with its Enforcement Decree (Presidential Decree No.
36053)
Jurisdiction & reach
Republic of Korea; extraterritorial — applies to foreign businesses whose AI activities affect the
Korean market and its users
In force
January 22, 2026 (Act and Enforcement Decree)
Regulator
Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT), with subordinate notifications and guidelines; the Personal
Information Protection Commission (PIPC) governs the parallel privacy layer
Architecture
Dual-purpose statute: industrial promotion (funding, talent, clusters) combined with trust
obligations on three categories — generative AI (advance notice and labeling), high-impact AI
(lifecycle safety, reliability, and assessment duties in domains including employment,
healthcare, financial services, public safety, and education), and high-performance AI
(additional safety duties above 10^26 FLOPs of cumulative training compute)
Penalties & posture
Administrative fines capped at 30 million won; MSIT is operating a grace period of at least one
year from entry into force, deferring fact-finding investigations and fines except in exceptional
cases involving serious social harm
Executive
implication
Compliance is a market-entry condition, not a penalty calculation: the duties apply now, the
grace period is prosecutorial forbearance rather than suspension, and the reputational lever —
in a market whose regulator has ordered remedies reaching models and data — exceeds the
fine schedule
8.2 Three categories, three duty sets
The Act’s operative logic is categorical, and an executive needs only the three categories. Generative AI carries
transparency duties: users must be notified in advance that they are dealing with generative AI, and AI-generated content
must be labeled, with the Enforcement Decree specifying the form and content of the disclosures. High-impact AI —
systems that materially affect life, safety, or fundamental rights in enumerated domains such as employment, healthcare,

financial services, public safety, and education — carries the heaviest duties: lifecycle risk management, impact assessment,
safety and reliability measures, and the ability to request MSIT confirmation of whether a given system qualifies. High-
performance AI, defined by the Decree’s compute threshold of 10^26 cumulative FLOPs — set roughly an order of
magnitude above the EU’s general-purpose systemic-risk threshold — carries additional safety obligations aimed at frontier-
scale developers, with safety-measure results submitted to the authorities.

8.3 The domestic representative and the foreign operator
The Enforcement Decree gives the Act an in-country enforcement anchor. Foreign operators meeting specified thresholds —
including one trillion won in total prior-year revenue, ten billion won in AI-service revenue, or an average of one million
daily Korean users over the preceding three months — must designate a domestic representative with a Korean address
or place of business, who represents and supports the operator in submitting large-scale AI safety-measure results,
requesting high-impact AI identification, and implementing high-impact safety and reliability measures. For a global
enterprise, this is Chapter 3’s accountability doctrine written into foreign statute: Korea has created a named seat of
accountability inside its borders and expects a person in it. Smaller operators below the thresholds are outside the
representative requirement but not outside the Act, whose substantive duties apply from the January 2026 date regardless.
8.4 The privacy layer: PIPA and the PIPC
Running in parallel with the AI Basic Act is Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act, substantially strengthened in
recent years, and its regulator, the PIPC — the authority that produced the Scatter Lab precedent of Chapter 1 and that has
since issued guidelines addressed specifically to generative AI development and deployment, functioning as a lifecycle
rulebook: legal-basis documentation for training data, pseudonymization expectations, input and output filtering controls,
and privacy-officer-led governance over generative AI systems. PIPA requires organizations to designate a privacy officer
responsible for data protection, giving the privacy layer its own named seat of accountability alongside the AI Basic Act’s
domestic representative. The practical consequence for program design is the same one Chapter 5 drew for Europe: the
privacy artifacts — lawful basis analysis, assessments, filtering controls — are the foundation on which AI Basic Act
compliance is assembled, not a separate workstream beside it.

Korea’s fines are small and deferred; its regime is neither. The duties are in force, the reach is extraterritorial,
the regulator has a documented record of remedies that reach models and data, and for the Korean market AI
governance is now a condition of entry.
