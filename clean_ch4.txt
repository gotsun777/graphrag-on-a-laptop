4.2 The calendar that matters
The Act’s obligations arrive in waves, and the July 2026 Digital Omnibus re-set only some of them. Three waves are already
operative. Since February 2, 2025, the Article 5 prohibitions apply — social scoring by public authorities, exploitative
manipulation of vulnerable groups, untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, emotion recognition
in workplaces and schools (with narrow exceptions), and related practices — alongside the Article 4 AI-literacy duty. Since
August 2, 2025, general-purpose AI model providers carry the obligations of Articles 51–56, supported by the
Commission’s GPAI Code of Practice published in July 2025. And from August 2, 2026, the Act applies generally, including
the Article 50 transparency duties: people must be told when they interact with an AI system, and AI-generated or
manipulated content — including deepfakes — must be disclosed and machine-readably marked.

What the Omnibus moved is the high-risk regime. Obligations for standalone Annex III high-risk systems — the employment,
credit, education, essential-services, and similar use cases — now apply from December 2, 2027; obligations for AI
embedded in products already regulated under EU product-safety law (Annex I) apply from August 2, 2028. The Omnibus
also added two new prohibited categories to Article 5 — systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery and child
sexual abuse material — with a grace period for the associated technical safeguards, and for the watermarking sub-
obligation for systems already on the market before August 2026, running to December 2, 2026.
The executive reading of this calendar is disciplined, not relaxed. The deferral was granted because the harmonized
standards that give high-risk systems a presumption of conformity were not ready — the requirements themselves did not
change, only the date on which they bite. An organization that treats December 2027 as a start date will attempt an
eighteen-month build in six; an organization that treats it as a completion date runs the Chapter 12 roadmap once, calmly,
and arrives with evidence in hand.
4.3 The high-risk regime as a build specification
For a company with any Annex III use case — hiring and worker management, credit scoring, education and vocational
access, essential public and private services, and the other enumerated domains — the high-risk chapter of the Act is best
read as an engineering and management specification with legal force. Providers must operate a risk management system
across the lifecycle (Art. 9), meet data and data-governance requirements for training, validation, and testing sets (Art. 10),
maintain technical documentation and automatic logging (Arts. 11–12), design for transparency to deployers and for
effective human oversight (Arts. 13–14), and achieve appropriate accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity (Art. 15), all
within a quality management system (Art. 17), completing conformity assessment and EU database registration before
placing the system on the market. Deployers carry their own duties under Article 26 — using systems per instructions,
assigning competent human oversight, monitoring operation, and retaining logs — and, for certain deployers, a
fundamental-rights impact assessment under Article 27.
Mapped onto the framework of Chapter 2, nothing here is exotic: Articles 9–10 are Know and Control at the data layer;
Articles 11–12 and 17 are Prove; Articles 13–14 and 26 are Own and Control at the point of use. The Act’s most common
misreading at executive level is role confusion — assuming that buying rather than building exempts the organization. It
does not: deployers have their own obligations, and a deployer that substantially modifies a high-risk system, or puts its
name on it, can become a provider with the full provider stack. Chapter 13 returns to this in the vendor context.
As of August 2026, the prohibitions, the AI-literacy duty, the GPAI obligations, and the Article 50 transparency
and labeling duties are live. Only the high-risk apparatus moved — to December 2027 and August 2028 — and
it moved because the standards were late, not because the requirements shrank.

4.4 The general-purpose AI layer
The Act regulates general-purpose AI models directly — a layer that matters to executives in two directions. If the
organization provides such a model, Articles 51–56 impose technical documentation, information for downstream providers,
a copyright policy, and a training-content summary, with additional systemic-risk obligations above the compute threshold
the Act sets for models with systemic risk. If — far more commonly — the organization builds on someone else’s model, the
same provisions define what documentation the organization is entitled to demand from its model vendor, which converts a
statutory obligation into a procurement checklist. The GPAI Code of Practice gives providers a compliance vehicle; a buyer’s
diligence question is simply whether its vendor has adhered to it, and what evidence accompanies the claim.
Deferring all EU AI Act work because “high-risk doesn’t apply until 2027.” The Article 50 transparency and
labeling duties apply from August 2026 to systems most enterprises already run — chatbots and generative
content pipelines — and the prohibitions have applied since February 2025. The deferred deadline is the
largest workstream, not the first one.
Which of our products will trigger Article 50’s disclosure and labeling duties as of August 2026, and has
anyone tested that the marking actually ships in production — not merely that a policy says it should?
