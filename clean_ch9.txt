mapped to all three regimes. This chapter argues — and shows — that the second is both cheaper and safer.

genuinely diverge; and the five design rules for a single global program.

9.2 Convergence, divergence, and the design consequence
Read down the columns and the convergence is unmistakable: every regime demands to know which systems make
consequential decisions, requires people to be told when AI is involved and content to be labeled, imposes heavier lifecycle
duties on high-stakes uses, reaches the frontier model layer, and names a seat of accountability. Those demands are the
Know-Own-Control-Prove framework restated three times in three statutory dialects — which is why Chapter 2 built the
program on the demands rather than on any one statute. The genuine divergences are narrower and manageable: timing

(Korea’s high-impact duties are live now, Europe’s high-risk regime lands in 2027–2028, the US state layer is in flux),
penalty philosophy (European turnover-based fines versus American uncapped enforcement versus Korean forbearance-
plus-corrective-power), and the American preemption fight, which has no European or Korean counterpart and requires
monitoring rather than building.
Five design rules follow for the single program. One inventory, classified once against the strictest applicable vocabulary,
with EU, US, and Korean tags derived from it rather than maintained separately. One integrated assessment per high-
stakes system — the GDPR DPIA extended with AI Act risk-management content, Korean high-impact assessment elements,
and US bias-audit evidence — completed at the deployment gate. One transparency implementation: design interaction
disclosure and content provenance once, to the union of Article 50, SB 942, and the Korean labeling rules, and ship it
globally, because maintaining jurisdiction-specific disclosure logic costs more than over-complying. One evidence layer
serving all regulators, because a document that satisfies the most demanding inquirer satisfies the rest. And one owner per
obligation across jurisdictions — the accountability map of Chapter 3 with three regulatory addresses, not three maps.

The three regimes differ in text, timing, and temperament, and hardly at all in anatomy. Classify once against
the strictest vocabulary, assess once per system, disclose once to the union of the labeling rules, file evidence
once, and name one owner per obligation. Jurisdiction-specific work then shrinks to a managed delta list.
Are we running one AI governance program with three regulatory mappings, or three programs with three
budgets — and if the latter, what would consolidation save, and who is accountable for proposing it?
